# Raw material for the line-by-line guide

Notes taken while filling the tool in as a self-employed house painter, tax year
2025, on 6 September 2026. **These are the questions I actually had with the
screen in front of me**, in the order I hit them — not a restatement of the IRS
instructions. Where I guessed, the guess and the reason are written down.

The through-line: *the tool sorts figures, it does not tell you which box a cost
belongs in.* That is the correct scope for the tool (`DECISIONS.md` §2) and it
is exactly why the guide page has to exist. Almost everything below is a "which
box" question, and the tool is silent on all of them by design.

---

## Before any line: the two questions at the top

**"How you count income" — When the money arrives / When the work is done / Something else.**
Best-written control on the page; nobody has to know the words *cash* and
*accrual*. The question I still had: **which one did I pick last year?** You are
meant to be consistent year to year, and nothing says so. A guide sentence:
*"Pick the same one you picked last year. If this is your first year, most sole
traders pick 'when the money arrives'."*

**"Did you work in this business regularly through the year?" — Yes / No.**
I ticked Yes in under a second. Then I wondered what it was really asking, since
it is line G, *material participation*, which has a stricter test than "regularly".
A painter with a day job who paints at weekends might tick Yes and mean something
the form does not. Worth a paragraph — this is the one question on the page where
the plain-English rewrite may be *too* comfortable.

---

## Part I — money coming in

### Line 1 · Sales and receipts
*"Everything the business was paid, including what came on a 1099."* — the single
most useful sentence on the page. It kills the commonest first-timer error, which
is to enter only the 1099 totals and leave the cash jobs out.

Questions I had anyway:
- **A customer paid a deposit in December for a job I finish in February. Mine this year or next?** Depends on the answer I gave two questions earlier, and the page does not connect the two.
- **Do card fees come off here, or go on an expense line?** I went with gross in, fee out as an expense — but I had to reason it out.

### Line 2 · Less: returns and allowances
*"Refunds and credits you gave customers."* A painter has no "returns". I put the
**$350 deposit I gave back** here and I am still not certain that is where the
firm would put it. Also unclear: a discount knocked off an invoice — is that an
allowance, or do you just invoice less and never mention it? (I would say the
latter, but the page does not say.)

### Line 6 · Other income
Left blank. The IRS label mentions **fuel tax credits**, which no house painter
has heard of and which is the only reason most sole traders would ever use this
line. The screen's rewrite — *"Anything else the business earned that is not a
sale"* — is friendlier and gives no hint of what actually belongs here.

---

## Part III — the fork that cost me the most time

### The "I buy or make things to sell, and I count stock" tick box
**This is the hardest decision on the page and it is a tick box with one sentence
under it.** I buy paint. Paint is a thing I buy. Is that stock?

I decided **no** — the paint goes on the customer's wall, I do not hold it to sell
— so my materials go on **line 22 Supplies**, not into Part III. I am fairly sure
that is right for a service trade, but I ticked the box first, typed my paint bill
into it, and had to back out. (Backing out is where the tool's worst defect lives:
see the defects note.)

What would have settled it in one line: *"If you had things sitting in a lock-up
on 31 December that you bought to sell on, this is you. If you buy materials and
use them up on jobs, it is not — put those on line 22."*

Sharpest confusion of the whole walk: **Part III line 38 is called "Materials and
supplies" and Part II line 22 is called "Supplies".** Two boxes, nearly the same
name, opposite answers for a painter, no sentence anywhere distinguishing them.
The guide has to draw that line explicitly.

### Lines 33 and 34
Never asked by the tool at all — and both are printed as **"Not answered"** in
every document it produces. If the guide covers them, say what they are for
(inventory valuation method, and any change to it) and that a service trade with
no stock skips them.

## Part II — money going out

### Line 8 · Advertising
Yard signs, a boosted post, the local paper. Easy. The one hesitation:
**van lettering — advertising, or a vehicle cost?** I put it on 8. (Signwriting is
generally advertising; the van itself is not.)

### Line 9 · Car and truck — the line that carries the most risk
The mileage helper is the best thing on the page: type miles, get a figure, and it
names *IRS Notice 2025-5* on screen and in the document. 9,240 × 70¢ = $6,468.

**The question a painter will actually ask: "does my van insurance go on 15 or 9?"**
And the answer nobody tells you: **if you take the standard mileage rate, the van's
insurance, fuel, servicing and repairs are already inside that rate.** You do not
also put them on 15 or 21. The tool will let you do it — it took my $1,860 of
insurance without a murmur — and that single mistake is worth more than every
other error on the form put together.

The helper says *"Only if you are using the standard mileage rate."* It does not
say the other half: *"and if you are, do not also claim fuel, insurance, servicing
or repairs anywhere else."* **That sentence belongs in the guide in bold.**

Two more, both real:
- **Van loan interest.** Still deductible (business share) on line 16b even when you take the mileage rate — the rate covers running costs, not finance. I had to know that; nothing on the page says it.
- **Commuting.** Home to the first job of the day: line 44b (commuting) or 44a (business)? It turns on whether your home office is your principal place of business — which is decided over on **line 30**, three sections away. The guide should tie 30 and 44b together explicitly.

### Line 11 · Contract labour and subcontractors
The two lads I sub out to on big jobs. Two questions, neither answered on screen:
- **Do I have to give them a 1099-NEC?** (Over $600, yes.) The tool nudges about Form 4562 on line 13 but says nothing about 1099-NEC here, which is the far commoner obligation.
- **Sub or employee?** If you set their hours and supply their kit, the IRS may call them an employee and this is the wrong line.

*(Note for whoever writes the page: the screen says "Contract labour". The
worksheet in the same PDF says "Contract labor". Pick the American spelling —
see the defects note.)*

### Line 13 · Depreciation and equipment written off
**The dead end.** I bought a sprayer for $1,150 this year. The label reads like
"equipment I wrote off", so my first instinct was to type 1,150 — and the tool
accepted it without a word. The hint says *"From your depreciation schedule or
Form 4562. This tool does not work it out."* Which is honest, and leaves a
first-timer with nowhere to go: **if you have never had a depreciation schedule,
you cannot produce this figure, and the tool cannot help you.**

What the guide has to answer: **is a $1,150 sprayer simply an expense, or does it
have to be spread over years?** (De minimis safe harbour, §179, bonus — that is
the whole question, and it is the most valuable paragraph the page could carry.)
I left the line blank and noted it for the preparer.

### Line 15 · Insurance
General liability, obvious. Van insurance, see line 9. And note: **the screen just
says "Insurance"** while the IRS label the worksheet prints is *"Insurance (other
than health)"*. The dropped qualifier is precisely the thing people get wrong —
your own health cover is not a Schedule C expense.

### Line 16b · Other interest
Van loan interest, $481.36. Question: **the whole payment, or just the interest?**
(Just the interest — the capital repayment is not an expense.) Bank charges are
not interest and end up in Part V.

### Line 17 · Legal and professional fees
Last year's accountant. Question: **the whole bill, or only the business part?**
If they did your 1040 as well, only the Schedule C share belongs here.

### Line 18 · Office expenses — versus line 22 Supplies
Printer ink and an invoice book on 18; masking tape and dust sheets on 22. Where
the line falls is not obvious and neither field carries an example. Second most
common "which box" question after 9-versus-15. And a laptop is neither — it is
line 13, which the tool will not do.

### Line 20a · Rent: vehicles and equipment
Scaffold tower and sprayer hire. Hesitation: **a van hired for a fortnight — 20a
or 9?** The two lines overlap for anyone who hires rather than owns.

### Line 21 · Repairs and maintenance
Servicing my own sprayer, new ladder feet. Two traps: **van repairs do not belong
here if you take the mileage rate** (again), and repairs you carry out *on a
customer's property* are the job, not an expense.

### Line 22 · Supplies
Paint, rollers, tape, dust sheets — $12,483.91, my biggest line. The whole
question is Part III versus here, above.

### Line 23 · Taxes and licences
Contractor licence renewal. This line attracts more wrong entries than any other
and the screen says nothing. **Not here:** your self-employment tax, your own
income tax, sales tax you paid on materials (that is part of what the material
cost). **Here:** business licences, and payroll taxes if you have staff.

### Line 24b · Meals (deductible part)
The helper is *"Take half of what I spent"*. I spent **$843.60** on food while
working; it gave me $421.80.

**Almost none of that $843.60 actually qualifies.** A painter buying his own lunch
on a local job is not a deductible business meal. The tool halves whatever you
type, and the caveat it prints is about the *percentage* (80% for drivers under
hours-of-service rules) rather than about *which meals count at all* — and it only
appears after you have already typed the number.

The guide sentence: *"First work out which meals had a business reason — a client,
or a trip that kept you away overnight. Ordinary lunches while working locally are
not deductible. Then halve that."*

### Line 25 · Utilities
Work phone and the lock-up electric. **"My mobile is also my personal phone —
all of it?"** No: business share only, and you should be able to say how you
worked the share out. Home electricity is not here — it is inside line 30 if you
claim the office.

### Line 26 · Wages
*"Wages you paid other people. Not money you took yourself."* **The best sentence
on the whole page.** What a sole trader draws out of the business is not an
expense, and this is the number one first-timer error. Steal that wording for the
guide verbatim.

### Lines 27a / 27b · Other expenses, and Part V
*"Things with no line of their own — software, bank charges, a licence. Schedule C
has room for nine; more than that goes on an attached list."* Clear. I listed
small tools $690, trade association dues $180, protective clothing $264.50.

Questions:
- **Protective clothing yes, ordinary work clothes no** — overalls and boots that could be everyday wear are not deductible. Worth its own line in the guide; painters ask this constantly.
- **Small tools versus line 13 again.** A $40 scraper is an expense. A $1,150 sprayer probably is not. Where is the line?
- **The line number moves.** Other expenses land on **27b for 2025** and on **27a for 2023 and 2024** — the IRS swapped them. Anyone copying figures off last year's worksheet will put them on the wrong line, and the guide should say so where a reader will hit it.

## Working from home

### Line 30 · Business use of your home
Square-foot method, 120 sq ft, $5 a foot, $600. The helper names the source and
caps at 300 feet.

The unanswered question is the qualifying one: **"regular and exclusive use."**
If the kids do their homework in the back bedroom, it is not a home office at all.
The tool asks how you want to work the number out; it never asks whether you are
entitled to one.

And the connection nobody makes: **if the home office is your principal place of
business, the drive to your first job of the day stops being commuting** — which
changes what you put in line 44b. Tie those two together on the page.

## What the form also asks

### Lines 43–47 · The vehicle questions
They appear only once line 9 has a figure, which is good. *"Miles getting to and
from work"* for commuting is friendlier than the form's wording. The catch is that
a painter driving from home to a job site does not think of that as "getting to
and from work" at all — see line 30 above.

### Line 32 · At-risk
Only shown when there is a loss. The wording — *"Was all the money in this
business yours to lose?"* — is excellent plain English for a genuinely hard idea.

---

## Where the guide should say the tool stops

The tool ends at **line 31, net profit**. What the reader wants next is *"so what
do I owe?"* — and self-employment tax is deliberately absent (`DECISIONS.md` §2:
*"It is not my concern to fill out a form for them… I would expect them to pay us
if they wanted to take it to that step"*). The guide should say that out loud
rather than let the reader wonder why the page stops where it does. **The refusal
is the offer.**
