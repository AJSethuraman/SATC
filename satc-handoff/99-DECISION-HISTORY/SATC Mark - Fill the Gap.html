SAT-C LLP · mark, round three

The gap isn't ours. It's theirs — and we're the piece that fits it.

Your read is better than mine. Nothing was removed by a razor; the square is the client's business, the gap is what's missing from it, and the oxblood piece is us.

Which means the mark now has two states, and the second one is the sale.

01

Two states, one geometry

Same square, same 8×8 piece, same path you already approved. The only variable is whether the piece is seated. Nothing new is drawn — which is why this works rather than feeling like a gimmick.

State 1 · Open

The gap, and the piece

The client's situation before you're involved: something missing, and the thing that fits it sitting apart. This is the working mark — the one on the nav, the card, the letterhead.

Use for Everything by default. Nav, business card, letterhead, favicon, signature, footer.
State 2 · Filled

Seated — and the seam still shows

The piece slots home. The square is complete, but the notch's inner edges remain as a visible seam, so you can still read that it arrived from outside. Completion without erasing the story.

Use for Engagement confirmed, statement paid, filing complete, "thank you" pages, the last slide of a proposal.
Why the seam matters. If the piece simply filled the corner with no seam, you'd have a plain square and the idea would vanish. The notch's stroke stays drawn over the seated piece — one line of SVG order — and that's the whole trick. Draw the piece first, the outline second.
02

Where the letters can and can't go

You wanted the letters connected to the piece. They can be — but only above about 64px. Below that a reversed letter inside an 8-unit square turns to mush, so the piece goes plain and the wordmark carries the name. Here's the honest cutoff.

72 · letter ✓

30 · plain ✓
20 · plain ✓
16 · favicon ✓
So the rule is simple: the lettered piece is an expanded mark for large applications only — report covers, the website hero, signage, a proposal title slide. Everywhere else the piece is plain and SAT‑C does the naming. Nobody learns a pronunciation from a 16px favicon; they learn it from the wordmark.
03

SAT-C leads now

The wordmark flips: SAT‑C is what people say, so it's what they see. The full legal name drops to the line beneath — still on every document, no longer the thing being read first. And the hyphen becomes the piece, tying the letters to the mark even when the mark isn't there.

SATC LLP

The hyphen device. Setting the hyphen in SAT‑C as a small oxblood square is the cheapest way to make the letters and the symbol feel like one system — it reads as a hyphen at a glance and as the piece on a second look. It also means the wordmark alone still carries the brand on a plain-text or single-colour application.

Don't animate the transition on the website. A logo that assembles itself on page load is a 2016 trope and it will date the site faster than anything else in the system. Use the filled state as a static mark on completion screens, where it means something.
What it means, in a meeting

"The square is your business. The gap is the part that isn't handled — the books, the filings, the thing you keep meaning to sort out. The piece is us. We're the shape that fits it."

Then, if it's going well, hand them the version with the piece seated. That's the close, and it doesn't need a word said over it.
