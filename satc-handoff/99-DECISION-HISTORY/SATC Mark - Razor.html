SAT-C LLP · mark exploration

Signify the cut, never the blade.

Occam's razor isn't about a razor. It's about removing what isn't necessary — so the mark should express subtraction and let the tool be inferred from what's missing.

All six keep your approved offset squares. Each is shown large, at 32px, at 16px favicon size, and inverted on navy — because a mark that only works big isn't a mark.

Read first

Why literal razors fail here. A drawn blade reads as a barbershop, and for a firm that handles client money it carries an edge of threat you can't design away. It's also detail-heavy — it turns to mush at 16px. Every option below is subtraction expressed geometrically: the razor is the thing that left, not a thing depicted.

A · The Channel

The gap it left behind

Your existing mark, with a precise negative-space channel where the two squares overlap. The razor passed through and left a clean seam — the cut is visible, the blade never is.

Strongest at small size: the channel keeps the two shapes legible at 16px where a plain overlap goes muddy.

B · The Chamfer

One corner pared off

Identical to A, except the far corner of the solid square is cut at 45°. A chamfer is a cut — it's the mark of a tool having been drawn across an edge, which is about as close to a razor as you can get without drawing one.

Quietest of the six. Reads as considered rather than clever — most people won't consciously notice, which may be exactly right.

C · The Trace

The path of the stroke

A single gold hairline crossing the whole mark and running past its edges — the trajectory the razor travelled. The most explicitly "razor" of the six without a blade anywhere.

Real risk: a diagonal through a square is also the universal prohibited symbol. Some people will read it as "no." That's a genuine hazard, not a nitpick.

D · The Notch

The piece removed, set aside

A square with a square bite taken out of it — and the removed piece sitting just outside, solid. The most literal statement of subtraction available in pure geometry: this came out, and here it is.

Best story of the six, and it survives 16px. Slightly busier silhouette — the step in the outline needs the stroke thickened at small sizes.

E · Three to One

Many resolve to one

Three squares stepping down, fading, until one lands solid. This is the only option that says the thing your homepage already says — three disciplines, one desk — and Occam's razor as reduction rather than cutting.

Watch: three elements is one more than a favicon really wants, and the opacity steps flatten out when printed in one colour.

F · The Half

One square, one clean cut

A single square divided exactly once. Nothing added, nothing decorative — which is itself the argument. If the razor says "don't multiply entities beyond necessity," this is the only mark that obeys it.

Most legible of the six by a distance, and unmistakable at 16px. Also the least distinctive — divided squares are common, so it leans hard on the wordmark to carry recognition.
Verdict

Take B, the Chamfer.

It keeps the mark you already approved, adds the cut as a single 45° pare on one corner, and never asks anyone to decode a metaphor. The razor is present in the geometry and absent from the picture — which is the only way this idea stays dignified on a document about someone's money.

If you want the concept legible to a client who asks "why the squares?", take D, the Notch — "we take out what isn't necessary, and we show you what came out" is a good answer in a meeting, and it earns its extra complexity.

I'd avoid C. The trajectory line is the cleverest of the six and the only one with a real failure mode: enough people read a crossed square as a prohibition sign that you'd be explaining it forever.

Rules if you develop any of these:

· The cut must be a single operation. Two cuts and the concept collapses into pattern.

· It must survive one colour. Test every candidate as solid navy on white before committing — opacity tricks won't print or emboss.

· Never add a blade, a handle, a glint, or a spark. The moment the tool is depicted, it becomes a barbershop.

· 16px is the real brief. If it doesn't hold at favicon size, it isn't the mark — it's an illustration.
