### D2 · Is a free FASB Accounting Standards Update acceptable authority where the Codification is licensed?

**Carried over.**

**What it means.** `DOMAINS.md` — the map of which publisher is competent to
settle which kind of question — names **`us-gaap`** as a body of authority and
gives **fasb.org** as its publisher. `SOURCES.md` admits **34 sources and not
one of them is US GAAP.** So `us-gaap` is a door with nothing behind it: a
question can classify into it, pass the publisher gate, and then find the record
holds nothing.

**The Codification** (the compiled, current, official statement of US GAAP) is
licensed — its content may not reach a model at all, which is why the record
marks it `human_only`. **An ASU** (Accounting Standards Update — the amendment
FASB publishes each time it changes a standard) is free to read on fasb.org.

**Measured:** **0 of the 98 recorded problems** classify `us-gaap` outright; 1
of 98 touches a second domain at all. So this costs almost nothing on the
recorded set. It matters on the **live** path — the lease question typed the way
an accountant types it is where `us-gaap` fires, and that is a real close
question.

**Either way:**
- **Yes** → `us-gaap` gets something to answer from, and lease questions stop
  reaching an empty body.
- **No** → `us-gaap` should come out of `DOMAINS.md` entirely, because a
  publisher the record admits nothing from is a promise the desk cannot keep.

**What I would do:** **yes, admit ASUs, tiered `secondary` and not `primary`.**
An ASU is the standard-setter's own published words, which is what makes it
authority; but it is an *amendment* and can be superseded by a later one, which
is what stops it being primary. The alternative — leaving a domain wired to a
publisher we hold nothing from — is the worse of the two.

### D4 · Should "this desk does not declare that source for this subject" refuse instead of warn? — NEW

**This is the decision Finding 3 actually bears on, and it is a real one.**

**What it means.** `SUBJECTS.md` records which source answers which subject. When
an answer cites a source the desk does *not* declare for that subject, the
engine prints a loud warning and **still serves the answer**.

**Finding 3, reproduced here exactly.** A deliberately wrong answer —
*"unidentified deposits are gross receipts and are booked to sales"* — citing
`26 CFR 1.263(a)-3(h)(3)(iv)`, with a genuine quote and a careless second reader
saying it supports. **It served, marked `primary · the firm treats as binding`.**

Every guard did fire: without a second reader it refused `not_judged`; it warned
that the desk declares S11/S26 for this subject and the citation came from S6;
it said one reader's yes is not a verification; and it printed the passage in
full. And the passage refutes the answer twice in its own words — it opens *"For
purposes of applying paragraph (h)(3)(i)"* (the safe harbour for **building
improvements**) and later says *"Gross receipts do not include the repayment of
a loan or similar instrument"*, which an unexplained deposit very often is.

**Measured, and this is the number Forge-Desk did not have.** I ran the warning
over all 98 recorded problems using each problem's *own recorded correct
citation*:

| how the question is phrased | right answers that would be refused |
|---|---|
| the problem's short title | **17 of 98** |
| the title plus its full facts | **0 of 98** |
| the facts alone | **0 of 98** |

So refusing would cost between 17 right answers and none, **depending entirely
on how wordy the question is** — and a bookkeeper's real sentence sits nearer
the terse end. That phrasing sensitivity is itself worth knowing and is new.

**Either way:**
- **Refuse** → Finding 3's answer never serves. Up to 17 recorded-correct
  answers stop serving too, and which ones depends on how the asker types.
- **Keep warning** → the wrong answer still serves, wearing a warning and its
  own refutation, and a reader who stops at the headline is taken in.

**What I would do:** **keep warning.** 17:1 at worst is a far worse trade than
the 4:1 you already declined, and the mitigation here is unusually strong — the
passage that refutes the answer is printed directly underneath it. But I would
**not** call this settled: the honest read is that the guard is
phrasing-dependent, and that is a defect in the guard rather than an argument
for either answer.

**What I would do:** **pair them.** It is deterministic, it reads from the firm's
own record rather than from a judgement, one pair fires today, and it closes the
gap without tuning any score. The `pool` docstring's rule — *the pool ranks and
does not choose* — survives: returning both is the opposite of choosing.

### D6 · Should a passage that says it applies somewhere else be marked as such? — NEW

**Finding 2, reproduced exactly — all six ranks matched.**

**What it means.** `26 CFR 1.263(a)-3(h)(3)(iv)` is headed *"Definition of gross
receipts"* and its first words are *"For purposes of applying paragraph
(h)(3)(i) of this section"* — the small-taxpayer safe harbour for **building
improvements**. It is a turnover threshold borrowed for one narrow purpose.
Asked *"are unidentified deposits gross receipts?"* it ranks **1**; Pub. 583, the
authority that actually reaches the question, ranks **5**.

**It is a class, not an instance. Measured:** **36 passages** open with a scoping
clause in their first 300 characters; **13 of those are definitions** (Forge-Desk
said 11 — see *What I got wrong*). Six probed all come back in the top 7 for the
very term they scope.

Nothing marks them. The scoping clause is in the stored text, so a careful reader
catches it — which is the design, and it is doing work.

**Either way:**
- **Mark them** → a passage whose own first line scopes itself carries a flag,
  printed above the passage: *"this passage says it applies for the purposes of
  [X]."* Nothing is demoted, nothing is scored differently.
- **Leave it** → the reader catches it or does not.

**What I would do:** **mark, do not demote.** Demoting would be tuning a ranking
by taste, which is the thing the pool was built not to do. Marking is a
deterministic read of the passage's own first sentence — the same kind of fact
the record already trades in.

### D7 · What should the brief do with worked examples? — NEW

**Finding 4, reproduced and slightly worse than reported.**

**What it means.** An **example** is a worked fact pattern the regulation prints
to show a rule applied — *somebody else's facts*. Examples are **260 of 786**
entries (33%) and they take **7 of 12** top slots on real working questions
(Forge-Desk measured 6 of 12; I get 7 on the same twelve). They are narrative and
concrete, so they share more words with a bookkeeper's sentence than an abstract
rule does.

Nothing in the engine knows that this client's paint booth is not Example 11's
paint booth. You already treat examples as special — `brief_for_grading` prints
none of them, so the corpus cannot leak its own answer key. The **live** brief
prints all of them, and the pool now ranks them first.

**Either way:**
- **Change it** → each example is labelled as another taxpayer's facts, and a
  brief is never allowed to be examples only: if every top hit is an example, the
  highest-ranked *rule* is pulled in beside them.
- **Leave it** → on roughly half of real questions the first thing the answerer
  reads is a fact pattern that is not this client's.

**What I would do:** **change it, both halves.** The label costs nothing and the
"never examples only" rule is what actually protects the answer — an example
read beside its rule is useful; an example read instead of its rule is a
confident wrong answer waiting to happen.

### D8 · Should the record hold what something cost? — NEW

**Finding 7, reproduced from the record itself.**

**What it means.** The desk declares exactly three fields it may record about an
engagement: **`capitalization_rule`, `trade`, `taxpayer`.** There is no field for
**what a unit of property cost** — in a record whose ratified positions are
stated in dollars ($2,500 and $5,000) and whose regulations turn on $200, $500
and $5,000.

Hit live on round trip `a276e4aed20a`: the asker supplied the per-unit cost, the
brief said *"NOT ON FILE and cannot be put on file"*, and Forge-Desk escalated
`no_field_for_this_fact` exactly as the brief instructs. Two of four purchases
were each under $200 in total, so no unit inside either can exceed $200 and
§ 1.162-3(c)(1)(iv) settles them on the definition alone. **That answer existed
and was thrown away at the door.**

**What it would cost, measured rather than estimated.** I added the field in a
scratch copy: it is **one word on one line of `corpus/SUBJECTS.md`**, no code
change.

```
**Records:** capitalization_rule, trade, taxpayer, unit_cost
```

**The risk, which is the part worth your attention.** Fact values are free text
— `value.strip()`, nothing else. So `unit_cost` would accept `$185`, `185.00`
and `one eighty five` alike, and a threshold question answered off a
mis-typed value is a wrong answer with a real number under it.

**Either way:**
- **Add it** → the $200, $500, $2,500 and $5,000 rules become answerable instead
  of refusing at the door. A format check has to come with it.
- **Leave it** → every dollar-threshold question escalates, which on a
  capitalisation-heavy close is most of them.

**What I would do:** **add it, with a format check in the same change** — a
value that is not a plain decimal amount is refused at the point of recording,
not at the point of answering.
